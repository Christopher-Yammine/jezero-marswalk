    • Surface measurements at Olympus Mons: none. Weather, dust, radiation and soil: not characterized.
    • Eruption forecast: none exists. No eruption has ever been observed, so no volcanic hazard is drawn.
    • Ground strength, rockfall, blocks under 1 m: not characterized.
    • Suit and habitat limits: unknown; the walk-back and slope limits on this page are placeholders until engineering reviews provide values.
    • Terrain model resolution: HiRISE DTM with 1 m posts, averaged to 30 m for routing; anything smaller is not resolved.

    Line of sight to the habitat antenna calculated

    Terrain can block radio. Clear means a straight line from a mast 3 m above the habitat scenario to a suit antenna 2 m above the ground misses the terrain, on the 30 m DTM grid with Mars curvature, out to 4 km from the habitat. It is geometry, not a signal measurement: it says nothing about link strength, and an orbiter relay is not modelled. Where the line crosses ground without elevation it is not characterized.

    Air pressure and radiation: inputs, no dose calculated

    Low ground has more air overhead, so higher pressure and somewhat more shielding; walls can also hide part of the sky. Neither pressure nor radiation has ever been measured at this site, and this app has no validated radiation model, so no dose number is computed. The measured references are from Curiosity at Gale crater: REMS surface pressure 7.32 to 9.25 hPa (daily values, first Mars year, sols 1 to 669, near the landing site at −4,503 m on MOLA) and RAD dose 0.21 mGy per day (Hassler et al. 2014), the figure the dose counters on this page use. Higher outside pressure does not change a suit's own pressure or its decompression risk: at about 1% of Earth's air pressure either way, a suit is still needed.

    Three separate claims

    • Mapped from orbit. The canyon is about 4,000 km long, about 200 km wide and up to about 7 km deep; Melas, Candor and Ophir chasmata are 5 to 7 km deep (ESA Mars Express HRSC, 2005). Landslides have left deposits on the floor; in the central chasmata material moved up to about 70 km from the northern scarp. This tile sits at a scarp the HiRISE team named "East Melas Chasma landslide scarp". Layered rock shows in the walls. orbital
    • Inferred about the ground. Most researchers think the canyon formed as a rift and was later widened by erosion and wall collapse; quakes or impacts are proposed triggers for the landslides. Whether any slope here is active today, how strong the debris is under a boot, and how big the blocks are below 1 m: not characterized. Debris aprons, collapse scars and boulder fields have not been mapped on this tile yet. inferred
    • Measured on the surface. Nothing. No rover or lander has worked in Valles Marineris, so there is no ground truth for weather, radiation, soil or rockfall.

    Phoenix: three separate questions

    • Is there buried ice? Yes, at the lander: the robotic arm uncovered a shallow ice table 5 to 18 cm down, in the centre and at the edge of a polygon (Smith et al. 2009). Measured at the lander only. Elsewhere on this map ice is likely shallow too (patterned ground, high hydrogen seen from orbit), but it is not measured along any route. observed at the lander
    • Are there visible ice-related landforms? Patterned ground covers the whole strip: polygons a few metres across, outlined by troughs, typical of ground with ice near the surface. They are smaller than the 30 m routing cell and everywhere, so they cannot steer a route; the 3 m slope grid catches steeper trough edges where the DTM resolves them. orbital
    • Can the ground be walked on? Partly characterized. Slope comes from a 1 m stereo DTM; the site is flat to gentle. Phoenix found indurated (crusted) surface soil and ice a few centimetres down; how either behaves under a boot is not characterized. calculated

    Utopia: three separate questions

    • Is there buried ice? Regional evidence. SHARAD radar on MRO found a subsurface deposit consistent with water ice across western Utopia, about 40 to 48°N and 73 to 93°E, holding about 14,300 km³ of water; where detected, the base of the ice-rich layer is 10 to 170 m down (NASA/JPL PIA21138; Stuurman et al. 2016). SHARAD resolves about 8 m vertically in ice, so it says nothing about the ground under a footstep. Shown as a regional outline, never as a walking hazard and never as a confirmed water supply. orbital / regional inferred
    • Are there visible ice-related landforms? Yes. The HiRISE image shows mantle with scalloped, terraced margins and rimmed depressions, landforms usually read as ice loss from the ground beneath. They were marked by eye and add route cost with a margin around them. A landform is visible evidence of past ice loss; it is not a measurement of ice today. orbital marked by eye
    • Can the ground be walked on? Partly characterized. Slope comes from a 1 m stereo DTM, which resolves scarps a walker would meet. Ground strength, crust, boulders under 1 m and buried ice under a route are not characterized. calculated

    Arcadia: three separate questions

    • Is there buried ice? Regional evidence only. SWIM 2.0 maps (Planetary Science Institute) are about 1° cells and say ice is consistent with the data, not measured here. Ice is likely near the surface, but its depth, quantity and access are not established along any route. It is not drawn on this map as a hazard or as a water supply. orbital / regional
    • Are there visible ice-related landforms? A candidate rampart crater (about 20 km, 43.70°N 212.27°E, about 140 km north of the habitat) has a lobate ejecta apron, which suggests volatile-rich ground, possibly ice, at impact. That is an interpretation of shape, not a detection of ice. No other ice-related landform was located in the walk area. orbital
    • Can the ground be walked on? Not characterized at walk scale. The inputs are a MOLA-calculated slope at about 463 m, an HRSC texture proxy for yardangs and a THEMIS night IR proxy for loose sand. None of these measures ground strength or footing. calculated orbital proxy
